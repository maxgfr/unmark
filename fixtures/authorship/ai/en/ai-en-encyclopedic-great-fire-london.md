The Great Fire of London was a major conflagration that swept through the central parts of the City of London from Sunday, 2 September to Thursday, 6 September 1666. It destroyed the medieval city inside the old Roman wall, consuming an estimated 13,200 houses, 87 parish churches, St Paul's Cathedral, and most of the buildings of the City authorities. The number of recorded deaths was small, though the true toll is uncertain, and it is thought that around 70,000 of the City's 80,000 inhabitants were left homeless.

The fire began in the bakery of Thomas Farriner on Pudding Lane shortly after midnight. A strong easterly wind spread the flames rapidly through the closely packed timber-framed houses, many of which had overhanging upper storeys and were roofed with thatch or tar-coated wood. A long dry summer had left the buildings ready to burn. The main firefighting technique of the period was to create firebreaks by demolishing buildings in the path of the flames, but the Lord Mayor, Sir Thomas Bloodworth, hesitated to order demolitions, and by the time action was taken the fire was out of control.

By Tuesday the fire had reached St Paul's, whose lead roof melted and ran through the streets. The wind dropped on Wednesday, and firebreaks created with gunpowder by the garrison at the Tower of London finally halted the spread.

In the aftermath, the City was rebuilt largely on its old street plan, but in brick and stone rather than timber, under regulations that shaped London's later development. Christopher Wren designed a new St Paul's and many parish churches. The Monument, completed in 1677, commemorates the fire near the point where it started.
