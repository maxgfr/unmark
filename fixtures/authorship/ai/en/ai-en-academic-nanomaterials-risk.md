Engineered nanomaterials are increasingly incorporated into consumer products, medical technologies, energy systems and industrial processes, yet the pace of their commercial adoption has outstripped the development of robust frameworks for evaluating their safety. This paper reviews selected aspects of the application of nanomaterials, the methodological challenges of assessing the risks they pose to human health and the environment, and the difficulties of communicating those risks to diverse audiences. We first outline the principal classes of nanomaterials in current use, including carbon-based structures, metal and metal oxide particles, and nanoscale polymers, and we highlight the physicochemical properties, such as size, surface area, shape and reactivity, that both underpin their technological value and complicate their toxicological characterisation. We then examine the limitations of conventional hazard and exposure assessment approaches when applied to materials whose behaviour depends on dynamic transformations in biological and environmental media. Particular attention is given to gaps in analytical methods, the scarcity of standardised test protocols, and the uncertainty inherent in extrapolating from in vitro data to real-world exposure scenarios. Finally, we consider risk communication as an integral component of responsible innovation. Evidence suggests that public perception is shaped less by technical estimates than by trust, perceived benefit and transparency about uncertainty. We argue that early and sustained dialogue among researchers, regulators, industry and citizens, combined with precautionary yet proportionate governance, is essential to ensure that the benefits of nanotechnology are realised while potential harms are anticipated and managed.
