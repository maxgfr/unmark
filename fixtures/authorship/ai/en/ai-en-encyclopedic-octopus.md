The octopus is a soft-bodied, eight-limbed mollusc of the order Octopoda. Around 300 species are recognised, and the order is grouped within the class Cephalopoda together with squids, cuttlefish and nautiloids. Octopuses are found in every ocean, from shallow coastal waters and coral reefs to the abyssal zone, and they occupy a wide range of habitats including rocky crevices, sandy seabeds and the open water column.

An octopus has a bilaterally symmetric body with two eyes and a beak-like mouth located at the centre of its arms. Lacking an internal or external skeleton in most species, it can squeeze through very small openings. Its arms are lined with suckers that are used for grasping, tasting and manipulating objects. Octopuses have three hearts, and their blood contains the copper-rich protein hemocyanin, which gives it a blue colour. Specialised skin cells called chromatophores, together with iridophores and leucophores, allow many species to change colour and texture rapidly for camouflage and communication.

Octopuses are considered among the most intelligent invertebrates. They have a large, complex nervous system, a substantial part of which is distributed through the arms. In laboratory and field studies, they have demonstrated problem-solving, observational learning and the use of objects such as shells as shelters.

Most species are solitary predators that feed on crustaceans, bivalves and fish. Defensive behaviours include ejecting ink, jet propulsion and, in some species, mimicry of other animals. Octopuses generally have short lifespans, often one to two years, and females typically die shortly after their eggs hatch. Several species are harvested commercially and are important in the cuisines of many cultures.
