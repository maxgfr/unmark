I was born into a world that seemed determined to overlook me, and yet I have never been able to persuade myself that I was wholly without value. It was in my tenth year, upon a cold afternoon in November, that I first understood how little consideration a poor and friendless child might expect beneath a rich relation's roof. The drawing-room fire burned brightly, and my cousins were gathered about it, warm and merry; but I, having been excluded from their circle with the remark that I must acquire a more sociable and childlike disposition before I could be admitted, retired to the window-seat and drew the heavy crimson curtain between myself and the company.

There I sat, with my feet tucked up beneath me, a volume of Bewick's British Birds open upon my knee. Beyond the glass lay a dreary prospect: a lawn sodden with rain, a leafless shrubbery bowing before the wind, and a sky of unbroken and mournful grey. I turned the pages, and let the pictures of far northern coasts and desolate churchyards take me from the room and from myself.

I had not been long thus employed when the curtain was violently drawn back. My cousin John, a boy of fourteen, stood before me, flushed with the consciousness of his own importance. "What are you doing behind there?" he demanded. "Come here and answer me." I obeyed, though with a heart that beat painfully; for I knew, by long experience, that obedience would purchase me no mercy.
